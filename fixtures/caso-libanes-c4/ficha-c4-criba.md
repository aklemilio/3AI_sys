# Task card — Cycle 4 — Blind screening of options (cultural preservation)

You are one of three independent screeners. You do not know who proposed each option and you will not see the other screeners. Your job is NOT to choose a winner. It is to (1) flag only options with a fatal flaw, (2) score every option, and (3) enrich the set.

## Objective being served
Preserve the spoken Lebanese cultural and linguistic heritage that is at risk of being flattened or lost, independently of whether large AI models learn Lebanese. Priority for people who do not read or write, low-income groups, rural and regional communities, older people, the diaspora and future generations.

## Rules
- Fatal flaw = the option cannot work or causes serious harm whatever the design. Difficulty, cost or risk that can be mitigated is NOT fatal. Each fatal vote needs support: "source" (URL plus exact short fragment, never invented) or "reasoning" (explicit premises).
- Every fatal vote must also propose a rescue: the change that would make the idea viable, or the alternative that keeps its intent.
- Score each option 1–5 on: impact (on the objective), feasibility (in Lebanon, now), evidence (strength of precedent or reasoning), safety (low risk to speakers and communities), inclusion (reaches the priority groups).
- Enrich: add up to 3 new options that nobody listed, or a combination of listed options that creates something new.
- Do not rank, merge or delete options.

## Options (41)

### C01 — National inventory of broadcaster archives
Create an inventory of audiovisual collections held by public and private television stations, national and regional radio stations, discontinued broadcasters and independent production companies. Document collection-level information about formats, periods, programme types, preservation conditions, ownership and accessibility without publishing recordings or personal data. Connect this inventory to existing preservation initiatives rather than assuming that preservation work must start from zero.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Broadcasters decline participation, inventories omit important collections, or information becomes outdated without an assigned custodian.

### C02 — Rights triage and clearance for archives
Reframe the bottleneck as legal uncertainty rather than technology. Sort every archive item into one of four buckets: over 50 years since broadcast; broadcaster-owned and under 50 years; contains performers or third-party works; unknown. Publish the register so that funders and users can act on the items that are already clear.
Variants proposed: Broadcaster Rights and Licensing Clearinghouse.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Broadcasters treat the register as a liability disclosure and refuse to cooperate, or ministerial turnover stalls the work.

### C03 — Rescue tapes at private and regional stations
Survey private TV channels, regional and defunct radio stations, and their retired technicians for surviving tapes. Offer free transfer in exchange for a preservation copy and a basic rights agreement. Give priority to formats at physical risk.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Owners fear losing commercial value, or tapes have already been recycled or discarded.

### C04 — Rescue household tapes and videos
Invite families to bring old VHS tapes, cassettes and reel recordings made from TV and radio, or of family gatherings, to mobile digitisation days. The family keeps the original and a copy. With consent, the archive keeps a copy for gaps in broadcaster holdings.
Variants proposed: Community Attics and Forgotten Recordings; Municipal Library Cassette and VHS Rescue Kiosks; Mobile Recording and Rescue Workshops.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Families fear that their private recordings will be exposed, or broadcasters object to copies of their programmes being kept.

### C05 — Preservation escrow without publication
Offer radio and television broadcasters contractual arrangements under which preservation copies are deposited with an independent custodian while ownership and publication rights remain separately governed. Establish permitted preservation operations, security arrangements, conditions for releasing material, succession procedures and responses to broadcaster closure. This route allows archival rescue without making public release a condition of participation.
Variants proposed: Broadcaster Dark-Archive Escrow and Orphan-Work Safe Harbor.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Deposit agreements do not authorize necessary copying and migration, succession clauses are ineffective, underlying rights remain unresolved, or the custodian cannot maintain the material.

### C06 — Distributed backup copies, including diaspora
Place encrypted preservation copies with partner libraries and universities in diaspora countries, held under deposit agreements that leave ownership and access control in Lebanon. This protects against war, fire and power failure.
Variants proposed: Distributed Heritage Vaults.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Sovereignty concerns block export, or partner institutions start treating the copies as their own.

### C07 — Minimum seed archive
Accept the no-intervention trajectory but secure a small, curated seed set: a few hundred hours covering each region, genre and decade, held in three locations. This keeps the option of later revival or study open at minimal cost.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Selection bias toward urban and elite material, or rights blocking copies abroad.

### C08 — Community guardianship licence
Adapt the Māori Kaitiakitanga licence model: speakers grant use for community benefit and education only, and surveillance or unconsented commercial training is excluded. A trusted custodian holds the data as a guardian, not as an owner.
Variants proposed: Kaitiakitanga-Style Sovereign Lebanese Voice Data License.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: There is no single legitimate community body in a plural society, or the licence cannot be enforced against foreign actors.

### C09 — Community-governed regional sound archives
Establish independent regional repositories governed by participating communities, with shared preservation services but local authority over access and interpretation. Permit public, community-only, restricted and temporarily closed collections. Draw on the FirstVoices model without assuming that Indigenous governance arrangements elsewhere transfer directly to Lebanon.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Local gatekeepers monopolize authority, repositories lack continuing resources, or restrictions are established without representative governance and review.

### C10 — Re-broadcast archives on radio and TV
Instead of putting the archive online first, schedule rights-cleared archive programmes on terrestrial TV and radio. Broadcast reaches older, low-income and rural audiences without internet access or literacy, and it is harder to scrape in bulk.
Variants proposed: Radio as a Living Heritage Channel.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: The public broadcaster lacks funds or a reliable power supply, or performer rights block re-broadcast.

### C11 — Local dialect community radio network
Inspired by Ireland's Raidió na Gaeltachta, establish low-power regional FM and internet radio blocks dedicated exclusively to unscripted local speech, storytelling, call-in debates, and folk music in regional Lebanese dialects without switching to Modern Standard Arabic or Beiruti media norms.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Broadcast licensing bottlenecks under Lebanese audiovisual media laws or advertiser pressure to standardize speech.

### C12 — Phone line to listen to archives
Set up a free or low-cost phone number with a keypad menu (by region, genre or decade) that plays short archive excerpts and lets callers leave a spoken comment or memory. It works on any basic phone and needs no reading.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Call costs, the operator's unwillingness to zero-rate the line, or no budget to curate the excerpts.

### C13 — Offline listening points
Provide free listening stations in libraries, municipal buildings, community centres and mobile cultural facilities. Organize selections by recognizable spoken themes and provide facilitators who can guide visitors without requiring reading, registration or a personal device. Use only recordings cleared for the relevant form of public or supervised listening.
Variants proposed: Solar-Powered Offline Village Audio Boxes.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Facilities are inaccessible, operating hours exclude intended audiences, equipment is not maintained, or rights restrictions prevent meaningful listening selections.

### C14 — Tiered online access shielded from harvesting
Reframe the archive as an asset that can be extracted. Serve audio by streaming only, with tiered access (public excerpts, registered full access, research agreements), explicit no-training terms, and self-hosting instead of large platforms. Bulk access is negotiated, not scraped.
Variants proposed: Community-Governed Online Listening Library; Broadcaster Archive Audio-Fingerprint and Watermark Shield.
Depends on AI: yes. Main risk raised: Determined scrapers bypass the controls, or the friction kills public use.

### C15 — Rights-cleared open heritage commons
Create a separate collection of cultural material whose contributors and other relevant rights holders deliberately authorize broad redistribution and reuse. Include commissioned performances, non-identifying cultural explanations and works whose rights status has been established. Explicitly distinguish material available for human cultural reuse from material additionally authorized for machine learning or other commercial purposes.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Permissions are incomplete, contributors misunderstand the practical irreversibility of broad redistribution, or copyright licenses are mistaken for sufficient authorization under privacy law.

### C16 — Collaborative cataloguing by listening
Run an online platform where volunteers anywhere transcribe and tag archive audio, in Arabic script or in an agreed Arabizi convention, and add place and context notes. This turns diaspora nostalgia into catalogue work.
Variants proposed: Listening-Based Community Cataloguing.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Inconsistent spelling, low volunteer retention, or rights holders refusing online exposure.

### C17 — Speech-recognition indexing of archives
Train a small speech-recognition model only on consented Lebanese speech, and use it to generate searchable transcripts of archive hours that humans could never transcribe by hand. The model stays under the custodian's licence and is not released for open reuse.
Variants proposed: AI-Assisted Archival Discovery; Dialect-Aware Automatic Alignment of Legacy Broadcasts.
Depends on AI: yes. Main risk raised: Too little consented data, poor accuracy on regional varieties, or pressure to release or sell the model.

### C18 — Voice search and interface for non-readers
Let users search the archive by speaking a question in Lebanese (for example, songs from the Bekaa in the 1970s) on a basic phone or a kiosk. The system replies with audio. It relies on locally governed speech tools (see O9).
Variants proposed: Conversational Search of Authentic Recordings; Acoustic-First Voice Archive for Non-Literate Users.
Depends on AI: yes. Main risk raised: Poor recognition of dialect variation, or queries being logged and reused.

### C19 — Measure where loss happens before acting
Test the premise. Commission a quick sociolinguistic map of the domains where Lebanese is expanding (talk shows, online chat, music) and those where it is shrinking (regional varieties, crafts vocabulary, rituals, minority languages). Redirect resources to the shrinking zones only.
Variants proposed: Measure Change Before Declaring Loss; Premise-Inversion Audit of Live Vernacular Vitality.
Depends on AI: yes. Main risk raised: The study is treated as an end in itself, or it is politicised along sectarian or regional lines.

### C20 — Sound atlas of regional varieties
If the real flattening is regional variation merging into a media koine, record a short standard word list and free conversation with elderly speakers in each municipality. Results become a public sound atlas held by municipalities and a national archive.
Variants proposed: Interactive Acoustic Dialect Atlas and Phonetic Comparison Map.
Depends on AI: yes. Main risk raised: Conflict in some regions, municipal paralysis, or elders declining to be recorded.

### C21 — Record crafts, work and daily life in context
Document spoken practices in their cultural settings rather than collecting isolated vocabulary or scripted sentences alone. Potential subjects include farming terminology, craft instruction, marketplace interaction, oral poetry, games, humour, lullabies and traditional forms of mediation. Let participating communities determine appropriate contexts, restrictions and whether documentation should occur at all.
Variants proposed: Occupational Soundscapes and Craft Lexicon Field Recording.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Recording changes the practice substantially, outsiders misinterpret its meaning, or communities regard documentation as extraction rather than preservation.

### C22 — Schoolchildren interview their elders
Model a scheme on Ireland's 1937 Schools' Collection: pupils record elders at home on a shared list of themes (sayings, work songs, games, remedies, place names). The pupil, family and school keep copies. Families may deposit them with an archive if they consent.
Variants proposed: School Oral-History Curriculum Without Written Exams.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Teacher overload and strikes, privacy fears, or sectarian sensitivities over some themes.

### C23 — School activities tied to home use
Learn from Irish revival efforts: school exposure alone does not produce daily use. Any school-based heritage activity must lead to use outside school, such as family listening assignments, neighbourhood performances or local radio slots run by pupils.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: The activities become a graded chore with no real-life setting.

### C24 — Language nests for young children
Create voluntary early-childhood and after-school environments where children encounter diverse Lebanese expressions through play, storytelling, movement and everyday conversation. Involve older speakers and community educators while maintaining respect for multilingual education and other languages used at home. Adapt the language-nest concept to transmitting cultural richness rather than assuming Lebanese requires language revival in the same sense as Māori.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: The programme turns into compulsory language instruction, promotes linguistic purity, lacks trained educators or excludes children because of income and location.

### C25 — Paid apprenticeships with tradition bearers
Pay elderly bearers (zajal poets, storytellers, work-song singers, craft speakers) a stipend to train young apprentices for a year. This treats bearers as teachers rather than as subjects to be recorded.
Variants proposed: Oral Heritage Apprenticeships; Live Zajal and Folk-Poetry Apprenticeship Stipends.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Few young people are interested, stipends stop after one funding cycle, or selection is politicised.

### C26 — Pay for living practice: zajal, storytelling, theatre
Fund regular, low-cost evenings of zajal, storytelling and sung poetry in villages, neighbourhood cafés and diaspora clubs, with young performers paired with veterans. The aim is living use, not recording.
Variants proposed: Pay for Living Cultural Practice.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Insecurity, the economic crisis cutting audiences, or the circles turning into folklorised staged shows.

### C27 — Protect gathering spaces
Reframe the heritage as the social practice (evening gatherings, village squares, cafés, seasonal festivals), not the recorded artefact. Support the times and places where people speak together, through municipal permits, small grants and safe venues.
Variants proposed: Preserve Spaces Where Lebanese Lives.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Depopulation of villages, insecurity, or the economic crisis closing venues.

### C28 — Family transmission with the diaspora
Provide free audio kits (stories, lullabies, games, everyday phrases) plus a weekly call routine pairing children abroad with grandparents or volunteer elders in Lebanon. The aim is oral use, without requiring the children to learn to read.
Variants proposed: Diaspora-to-Village Voice-Note Oral History Circles.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Children lose interest, time zones get in the way, or there are child-safety concerns with volunteer pairings.

### C29 — Intergenerational family circles without recording
Support recurring household storytelling, shared meals, oral games, songs and conversations between generations. Offer optional spoken prompts through radio or community educators without requiring families to record, upload or submit evidence of participation. Allow each household to choose its own linguistic practices and traditions.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Participation feels compulsory, households lack time or willing participants, or family structures are assumed to be uniform.

### C30 — Family recordings kept at home
Distribute simple guides, such as a printed card or a voice message, to help families record their elders on their own phones and store the recordings in the family, with no central repository. Families can later choose to deposit them.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Recordings are lost when phones break, or they are uploaded to platforms that harvest them.

### C31 — Proverb and recipe decks for family tables
Publish illustrated, phonetically intuitive card decks and audio-QR booklets of hyper-local proverbs, lullabies, curses, and seasonal food idioms collected within extended families, designed for dinner-table games across generations in Lebanon and the diaspora.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Sanitizing earthy, humorous, or politically sharp folk expressions into bland tourist souvenirs.

### C32 — Diaspora as its own reservoir
Treat diaspora communities as contributors to Lebanese spoken heritage rather than only recipients of material produced in Lebanon. Establish intergenerational exchanges, travelling cultural programmes and community-led documentation of diaspora expressions. Allow diaspora varieties to enter collections on their own terms instead of correcting them against a homeland standard.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Diaspora varieties are treated as defective, programmes favour affluent expatriate networks, or cross-border recording and transfer obligations remain unresolved.

### C33 — Include Lebanon's other spoken heritages
Widen the scope to Armenian, Syriac and Aramaic liturgical speech, Kurdish, Domari and Lebanese Sign Language, along with community-specific registers. Ask each community what it wants preserved and who should hold it.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: The communities distrust state or foreign projects, or there is political sensitivity around some groups.

### C34 — Legal reform: preservation exception and orphan works
Propose amendments to Law 75/1999: a preservation copy exception for archives, an orphan-works regime for recordings whose rights holders cannot be traced, and a clear rule for folklore-based recordings. This removes legal fog for every actor at once.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Parliamentary paralysis, and lobbying by rights holders.

### C35 — Single oral-first consent protocol
Write one plain-language, oral-first consent protocol that satisfies both GDPR (explicit, granular, revocable consent; no speaker identification unless agreed) and Lebanese law, so that foreign-funded projects do not each improvise. It includes a version read aloud for non-readers.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Funders' lawyers reject a shared template, or the protocol becomes too heavy for fieldwork.

### C36 — Voice data hosted in Lebanon, federated access
Keep raw identifiable voice recordings and spoken consent records stored on encrypted servers physically or legally governed in Lebanon under community trusteeship, while exposing only anonymized, consent-verified metadata or aggregated linguistic features to EU-funded international partners.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Power and connectivity outages in Lebanon making locally hosted pods unreachable without resilient solar-backed mirror nodes.

### C37 — Minimum intervention with periodic observation
Leave selected cultural practices free of organized recording, formal teaching and promotional intervention while periodically examining aggregate indicators of their continued use. Establish community-controlled opportunities to request assistance if conditions change. Preserve existing non-identifying evidence of cultural activity without obligating communities to become archival subjects.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Observation misses irreversible losses, communities cannot communicate changing needs, or the absence of measured decline is mistaken for evidence of continued vitality.

### C38 — Deposits for future generations
Invite communities and institutions to create deliberately selected deposits intended for access or reassessment over the next several decades. Include contextual explanations of contemporary speech, cultural changes and the reasons for selecting particular materials, without publishing identifying contributor information. Establish legally reviewed succession, access-review and withdrawal arrangements rather than promising permanent confidentiality or automatic future publication.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Future custodians cannot interpret the deposits, access conditions become legally or technically impossible to administer, or the institution responsible for succession ceases to operate.

### C39 — Preserve contemporary digital speech culture
Establish voluntary deposit arrangements for Lebanese podcasts, online comedy, independent video productions, spoken social media content and other contemporary cultural expressions. Accept authorized preservation copies directly from creators rather than indiscriminately harvesting publicly available content. Include contemporary multilingual and experimental material alongside older forms.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: Creators cannot authorize all incorporated material, platform popularity determines archival selection, or the preservation process exposes participants who expected limited circulation.

### C40 — Acoustic restoration of damaged tapes
Apply specialized audio-restoration signal processing to remove wow-and-flutter, tape hiss, hum, and print-through from degraded radio dramas, political speeches, and folk broadcasts, preserving both the raw archival master and a clean listening master.
Depends on AI: yes. Main risk raised: Over-processing audio until subtle dialectal consonants are distorted or discarding unedited raw transfers.

### C41 — Collective royalties for archive and voice use
Create a collective licensing clearinghouse that charges commercial media producers, filmmakers, and vetted language-technology licensees a usage fee for authenticated Lebanese archival and community speech, routing micro-royalties back to surviving performers, rural contributors, and broadcaster preservation funds.
Depends on AI: no. Main risk raised: High administrative overhead consuming royalty pools or lack of mobile-cash payout mechanisms in rural areas.
